### Q1 `[Firefly]` `[positioning]`
**Q:** How does Mike describe Firefly's strategic positioning?

**A:** (From Adobe blog posts and Sharebird context) Mike Polner has written that Adobe Firefly's goal is to give creators "one place where you can choose the right industry-leading model for your project." The positioning emphasizes being the creative AI that is both powerful AND safe for commercial use — unlike Midjourney or DALL-E, Firefly is built on content Adobe has the rights to, making it "commercially safe."

---

### Q2 `[consumer_pmm]` `[career]`
**Q:** There has been a lot of discussion about Consumer Product Marketing as a discipline. Can you speak to the shift you've seen?

**A:** There has been a massive shift in awareness and momentum around Consumer Product Marketing overall. When Mike joined Uber Eats, he was among the first Consumer PMMs in that role. The discipline is now recognized as distinct from B2B PMM — it requires understanding growth loops, virality, and consumer psychology in ways that don't translate directly from enterprise PMM playbooks.

---

### Q3 `[product_launch]` `[metrics]`
**Q:** What KPIs do you set for a product launch?

**A:** Set one primary goal with secondary goals beneath it. One underrated KPI he recommends considering: "number of fires" — meaning how many unexpected problems surface post-launch. Fewer fires means better internal alignment, better QA, and a smoother customer experience. He also recommends tiering launches so you don't treat every update as a Tier 1 event.

---

### Q4 `[team_building]` `[career]`
**Q:** When hiring consumer product marketers, what do you look for?

**A:** Background is not as important as skills. He hires for signal on skill rather than whether someone has held the role before. Key skills: the ability to think like a consumer, comfort with data and growth metrics, and the ability to ship fast and iterate. Consumer PMM is closer to growth marketing than traditional enterprise PMM.

---

### Q5 `[product_launch]`
**Q:** What tools/systems do you use to manage product launches?

**A:** He is a Google Docs power user. The simplicity of a shared doc that everyone can comment on beats over-engineered project management tools for most launch coordination. Keeps the GTM checklist, messaging doc, and launch brief all in Drive.

---

### Q6 `[Firefly]` `[positioning]`
**Q:** (From Adobe context) How does Adobe communicate Firefly's value to creators vs. enterprises?

**A:** (Synthesized from Adobe blog/search context) The B2C message focuses on creative empowerment and speed — "make anything you can imagine." The B2B/enterprise message (Firefly Services, Custom Models) focuses on brand consistency and content production at scale. Mike's team (Next Gen Creators) owns the B2C creator narrative. ## Katharine Gregorio `[Director+]`

**Sr. Director, Product Marketing — Creative Cloud — Adobe**

[Profile on Sharebird](https://sharebird.com/profile/katharine-gregorio)
[Adobe PMM Team page](https://sharebird.com/c/adobe/product-marketing)

*Oversees all Creative Cloud apps: video, imaging, design categories. Firefly is integrated across CC, making her team directly involved in how Firefly features are messaged within CC products.*

---

### Q1 `[positioning]` `[messaging]`
**Q:** What is the difference between positioning and messaging, and how do you use each?

**A:** Positioning is an internal artifact. Messaging is externally facing and brings positioning to life in various contexts. Positioning is what your company uniquely provides a specific audience in a particular market — it's the foundation. Messaging is how you translate that positioning for specific channels, audiences, and moments. They're different documents with different audiences: positioning is for internal alignment; messaging is for the market.

---

### Q2 `[messaging]` `[team_building]`
**Q:** What messaging framework do you use to get executive buy-in?

**A:** She uses the tried-and-true umbrella message with 3 pillars and proof points. The umbrella is the one-line value proposition. Each pillar supports it with a distinct angle, and each pillar has 2-3 proof points (data, customer quotes, feature specifics). This structure is predictable for executives, easy to pressure-test, and maps naturally to creative brief structures.

---

### Q3 `[positioning]` `[messaging]`
**Q:** How do you handle solution messaging for multiple products that share a platform?

**A:** Core platform messaging serves as an input to solution messaging. Solution messaging focuses on personas and how they use your product to help them do something better, smarter, faster, cheaper. The platform message creates the umbrella; each solution message inherits it but sharpens for a specific use case and buyer. At Adobe Creative Cloud, this means the "all your creative tools, one subscription" platform narrative filters into specific Photoshop, Premiere, Illustrator messages that each have their own persona focus.

---

### Q4 `[research]` `[messaging]`
**Q:** How do you test your messaging?

**A:** Work with Sales and Customer Success teams to reach out to customers for feedback on messaging via phone or video calls. Offer incentives if needed. The goal is qualitative signal before investing in creative — does the message land? Does it prompt the right follow-up questions? She treats messaging testing like early-stage user research: small sample, high fidelity, directional rather than statistically significant.

---

### Q5 `[team_building]` `[positioning]`
**Q:** When you join a new PMM role, what are the top 3 things you audit first?

**A:** (Katharine has answered this type of question on Sharebird.) Look at: (1) existing personas and whether they reflect who is actually buying/using the product today, (2) the messaging framework and whether it's consistent with how Sales talks to customers, (3) what launches have happened in the last 12 months and whether they had a coherent strategy or were reactive. These three audits tell you whether you have a foundation to build on or need to start over.

---

### Q6 `[team_building]`
**Q:** How do you divide responsibility between the PMM team and the campaign team?

**A:** PMM owns the strategy, positioning, and messaging — the "what we say and why." Campaign owns the creative execution and channel strategy — the "how we say it and where." The PMM team inputs a messaging brief; the campaign team produces assets. Where it breaks down is when PMM tries to own creative or campaign tries to own messaging. The division works when both sides respect the handoff point.

## Gagan Mand `[Director+]`

**Director, Product Marketing & Strategy — Adobe**

[AMA: GTM Strategy & Product Launches for Enterprise Software](https://sharebird.com/h/product-marketing/ama/adobe-director-product-marketing-strategy-gagan-mand-on-gtm-strategy-and-product-launches-for-enterprise-software)

*Focuses on enterprise (Digital Experience products including AEM, Marketo, Analytics). Has led GTM for Adobe Experience Manager and the first enterprise trials for Adobe's Digital Experience suite.*

---

### Q1 `[team_building]` `[roadmap]`
**Q:** How do product marketers, PMs, and Engineering work together?

**A:** He describes them as "the three legs of a stool" — each plays a crucial role in keeping the business and product stable. PMM brings the market perspective (what customers need, what competitors are doing, what messaging lands). PM brings the product perspective (what's feasible, what's prioritized). Engineering brings the build perspective. Remove any one leg and the stool falls. The key failure mode is when PMM is brought in too late — after the product is built — and has to message around constraints instead of shaping strategy.

---

### Q2 `[product_launch]` `[sales_enablement]`
**Q:** How do you roll out new collateral and training to a remote sales team?

**A:** Use scalable channels: Sales/Partner Enablement Platforms (like Highspot or Seismic), Live Enablement Webinars, and Demo Hubs. The key is modular content — short-form assets that Sales can grab and customize, not 40-slide decks. For enterprise software, he emphasizes building a "message hierarchy" so Sales always knows which elevator pitch maps to which persona and stage of the sales cycle.

---

### Q3 `[roadmap]` `[team_building]`
**Q:** How do you work with the Product/Engineering team to understand the pipeline of product releases?

**A:** Establish a regular cadence with PM — at Adobe, this means quarterly roadmap reviews plus monthly syncs. The PMM should be in product planning meetings early enough to influence what gets built and how it gets framed. He recommends PMMs develop enough technical fluency to understand what's shipping, not just read the release notes.

---

### Q4 `[product_launch]` `[metrics]`
**Q:** What are the most important launch metrics for enterprise software?

**A:** Pipeline influence and sales velocity are the top two. For awareness launches, track SOV (share of voice) and analyst coverage. For product-led growth components, track trial-to-paid conversion. He also tracks "sales confidence" — anecdotally, do reps feel equipped to talk about the new feature? This is a leading indicator of whether the launch will actually move pipeline.

---

### Q5 `[team_building]`
**Q:** What capabilities are essential vs. nice-to-have when building a PMM function?

**A:** Essential: positioning/messaging, sales enablement, product launch process. Nice-to-have (until you scale): competitive intelligence (can be shared with marketing), analyst relations (often owned by comms), customer evidence/references (can be owned by CS). Start with the essentials and add specialization as the team grows. At Adobe's scale, every PMM sub-function is staffed; at earlier stages, PMMs wear multiple hats.

*Has managed 250+ product launches throughout her career. Started at Google as Account Manager; leads Lightroom PMM at Adobe.*

---

### Q1 `[product_launch]` `[metrics]`
**Q:** How do you set launch goals and KPIs?

**A:** Every launch should have one "north-star" goal and cascading KPIs — and this varies by launch. The north-star could be increasing Annual Recurring Revenue (ARR), increasing customer Monthly Active Use (MAU), or increasing net new customers. Once you've landed on that goal, be ruthless about developing a GTM strategy and choosing metrics to track progress against it. Don't track everything; track what the north-star demands.

---

### Q2 `[product_launch]` `[roadmap]`
**Q:** What launch artifacts do you create for a Tier 1 launch?

**A:** Four core documents:
1. **GTM checklist** — the "Bible" of the launch; includes metrics, links, all key drivers, owner names, and status
2. **Launch brief** — for creative teams (in-house or agency); captures the audience, goal, and creative direction
3. **Product communication doc** — feature specs, talking points for Sales/CS, FAQ
4. **Messaging document** — the core message behind what's being launched, the "why this, why now"

For Tier 2/3 launches, she scales down — maybe just the GTM checklist and messaging doc.

---

### Q3 `[roadmap]` `[product_launch]`
**Q:** How do you assess strategic readiness before building a GTM plan?

**A:** Before building the plan, answer these questions: Who is the product for? What problem does it solve? How does this fit your company's positioning? Have you assembled the core internal GTM team (PM, PMM, Design, Sales, CS)? What's your launch tier and why? Only after answering these do you build the execution plan. Starting with tactics before you have strategic clarity is the most common GTM mistake.

---

### Q4 `[career]` `[team_building]`
**Q:** What does it take to be a stellar product marketer?

**A:** Key traits she looks for: super organized (project management experience is a signal), scrappy (can try new things with a limited budget), great collaborators (can lead and influence without authority), and customer-obsessed. At more senior levels, she adds: strategic thinking (connecting individual initiatives to company goals) and executive presence (can represent PMM in leadership forums).

---

### Q5 `[career]`
**Q:** Tips for answering "Tell me about a successful product launch you led" in a PMM interview.

**A:** Break it down into components rather than telling a linear story:
1. Explain the product and its market purpose — why it exists
2. Describe the planning and execution process from ideation to launch day
3. Discuss challenges and how you overcame them (this is where interviewers lean in)
4. Share results achieved and customer feedback

The best answers make it clear what *you specifically* did vs. what the team did, and tie results to business metrics (not just "it went well").

---

### Q6 `[team_building]`
**Q:** How do you build a PMM team from scratch?

**A:** Start with hiring generalists first — "full stack PMMs" who can do positioning, launch, and sales enablement. Add specialists (competitive, research, content) once you have enough volume to justify the specialization. For the first hire, look for someone scrappy who has launched products before — not someone who has only worked in large PMM orgs with highly divided responsibilities.

---

### Q7 `[product_launch]` `[career]`
**Q:** How do you tier your product launches?

**A:** Tier 1: major releases that affect significant revenue or the entire customer base — full launch treatment, CEO/PR involvement, cross-functional GTM team. Tier 2: meaningful features or market expansions — PMM-led with support from sales enablement and content. Tier 3: minor updates — changelog entry, maybe an email, no external press. The discipline is in not letting everything become Tier 1 through organizational pressure.

---

### Q1 `[roadmap]` `[team_building]`
**Q:** What is the best approach to influencing C-suite executives in a business strategy decision?

**A:** It comes down to ensuring they understand the role AND value that product marketing offers. C-suite stakeholders respond to business outcomes, not PMM activities. Frame your work in terms of pipeline, retention, or revenue impact — not messaging frameworks or launch checklists. Find an ally in a peer department leader (often Sales or Product) who already has C-suite credibility and who understands PMM's value. Use that ally to build your case before the room.

---

### Q2 `[team_building]` `[career]`
**Q:** How do Product Marketers act as ambassadors across departments?

**A:** Product Marketers act as the connective tissue — they bring customer and market insights to Engineering (so the product team understands "why"), they bring product clarity to Sales (so reps can speak confidently), and they bring revenue context to leadership (so the C-suite understands what's working). The ambassador role means you are always translating: translating customer pain into product requirements, and translating product capabilities into customer value.

---

### Q3 `[messaging]`
**Q:** How important is it to identify the impact of your messaging, and how do you measure it?

**A:** Incredibly important — but it doesn't have to be purely quantitative. The business needs to know if there are positive business returns on messaging work. Qualitative signals (Sales says prospects respond positively to X message, win rates improve in deals where Y positioning is used) are valid data. Quantitatively, look at conversion rates at the top of funnel, trial-to-paid conversion, and deal velocity when specific messaging is used vs. not.

---

### Q4 `[roadmap]` `[team_building]`
**Q:** "GTM Strategy" means different things to different people. How do you define it and align on it internally?

**A:** GTM strategy can mean a lot of different things — launch plan, market entry plan, annual marketing plan. Getting clarity on definition is step one. His framework: GTM strategy = who you're selling to (ICP), what problem you're solving (positioning), how you reach them (channels + motion), and how you measure it (leading + lagging KPIs). Everything else is tactics that serve one of those four elements. When internal debate happens about "GTM strategy," map the debate back to which of those four elements is actually in question.

---

### Q5 `[roadmap]` `[metrics]`
**Q:** Who are the key stakeholders when aligning on a GTM strategy?

**A:** Prioritization is the most consistent theme — not everyone needs to be in every conversation. Core team: PMM, PM, Sales leadership, Marketing. Extended: Finance (for pricing/packaging), CS (for post-sale motions), Comms (for PR timing). The mistake is over-including stakeholders early and under-including them late — getting sign-off from Sales leadership before launch day, not after.

---

### Q6 `[messaging]` `[competitive]`
**Q:** How do you approach competitive positioning for a region like APAC where the competitive landscape differs from the US?

**A:** (From LinkedIn/Sharebird context) The global positioning framework is a starting point, not a destination. In APAC, competitor strength varies significantly by country — a competitor dominant in Australia may be irrelevant in Southeast Asia. Jeremy's approach: maintain the global positioning foundation but develop regional battlecards that reflect who you actually lose deals to in each market. Work with regional Sales to identify the real competitive threats, not the ones on the global battlecard.

## Summary: How Adobe PMMs Think

Across all five contributors, consistent themes emerge:

**On positioning & messaging:**
- Positioning is internal (strategy); messaging is external (execution)
- Use the umbrella + 3 pillars + proof points framework to get executive buy-in
- Test messaging qualitatively with customers before scaling creative

**On product launches:**
- Tier every launch — not everything is Tier 1
- North-star goal first, then cascading KPIs, then tactics
- Four core launch artifacts: GTM checklist, launch brief, product comm doc, messaging doc

**On Firefly specifically** `[Firefly]`:
- Owned by Mike Polner's team (VP PMM & GM, Next Gen Creators)
- Key differentiator: "commercially safe" AI (trained on licensed/Adobe Stock content)
- B2C positioning: creative empowerment for individual creators
- B2B positioning: content production at scale, brand consistency (Firefly Services/Custom Models)
- 4.5B+ generations cited as social proof of adoption
- Firefly is positioned as an "all-in-one creative AI studio" — multi-model, creator-choice

**On the Director of Marketing role at Adobe:**
- Director-level PMM at Adobe = highly strategic; managing cross-functional alignment across large orgs
- Directors own processes and partnerships, not just individual deliverables
- Key Director skill: knowing what questions to ask, not having all the answers
- Directors connect their work to outcomes outside their function (revenue, retention, pipeline)
- At Adobe, PMM Directors typically own a product family (e.g., Lightroom, Firefly, CC apps) with several IC PMMs reporting in
